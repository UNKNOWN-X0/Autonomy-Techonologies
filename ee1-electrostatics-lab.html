Analogy

Euclid's geometry

Geometry starts from a few postulates (“a straight line can be drawn through any two points”), adds definitions (“a circle is…”), and proves everything else as theorems. Electrostatics is organised the same way: three axioms, a handful of definitions, and every formula in the chapter is a theorem you could derive.

GeometryElectrostatics postulates→charge, Coulomb, superposition definitions (circle, angle)→E, φ, U, D, C theorems (Pythagoras)→Gauss, interface conditions, C = εA/d

Holds: you only memorise the base; everything else can be rebuilt by reasoning. If you forget a capacitor formula, derive it.

Breaks: Euclid's postulates are chosen, so they can't be wrong. Physical axioms are measurements: a new experiment could overturn them. That is why they're called axioms “from experiment”.

Part 1 · Notes 1.1–1.2

Charge and Coulomb's law

Axiom

Charge comes in two kinds and in whole multiples of e. Two point charges at rest push or pull each other along the line joining them, with a force proportional to both charges and to 1/r2. This is measured, not derived: everything else in the chapter is built on it.

Fon 2 = 14πε0 · Q1Q2r2 · erer : unit vector from Q1 (source) to Q2

Drag either charge and change the charges. Watch three things: the sign of Q1Q2 alone decides attract or repel; the two forces are always equal and opposite; and the size falls as 1/r2.

The curve is |F| against r for the current charges. The filled dot is your current distance. The hollow dot sits at twice that distance: its force is exactly one quarter. Arrow lengths on the bench above use a compressed (logarithmic) scale so both small and large forces stay visible.

Try this. Note the force, then press “Flip every sign”. Nothing changes, because only the product Q1Q2 appears in the law. That is the whole reason calling glass “positive” is an arbitrary convention.

Key facts

  • Charge is a whole multiple of e = 1.602·10−19 C and is conserved. Rubbing moves electrons; it never creates charge. [1.1]
  • The sign of Q1Q2 decides the direction: positive repels, negative attracts. No separate rule is needed.
  • The unit vector er points from the charge causing the force to the charge feeling it.
  • Action = reaction: the two forces have equal size and opposite direction.
  • Valid for point charges (size ≪ distance), at rest, in vacuum. 1/(4πε0) ≈ 8.99·109 Vm/(As).
Analogy

Charge is money in bank accounts

Rubbing glass with wool is a bank transfer: electrons move from the glass's account to the wool's. The glass ends up “in credit” (+), the wool “in debt” (−), and the bank's total is unchanged. Nobody prints money.

BankCharge transfer between accounts→rubbing moves electrons total money in the bank is fixed→charge conservation smallest coin: 1 cent→elementary charge e credit / debt→positive / negative

Holds: conservation and quantization. You can't own half a cent, and you can't hold 0.5 e.

Breaks: a debt is just a missing amount, but negative charge is a real particle (the electron) sitting there. And money in accounts doesn't push other accounts around.

Analogy

Coulomb's law is Newton's gravity with two signs

Put the two laws side by side and they have the same skeleton: a constant, the product of two “amounts”, divided by r2, along the line between them.

Gravity F = G · m1m2r2Coulomb F = 14πε0 · Q1Q2r2

Holds: the 1/r2 shape, the force along the connecting line, action = reaction, the product of the two amounts. Everything Part 5 says about spheres works for gravity too.

Breaks: mass has only one sign, so gravity only attracts. Charge has two, so electric forces can repel and can cancel. And the strength differs hugely: between two protons the electric push is about 1036 times the gravitational pull.

Analogy

Flipping every sign is swapping the colours in chess

Paint every white piece black and every black piece white. Every move, every capture, every checkmate is exactly the same game. The colours were labels; only “same side” versus “opposite side” mattered. Likewise only “same sign” versus “opposite sign” (the sign of Q1Q2) matters in Coulomb's law.

Holds: the labels carry no physics; only the relation between two pieces matters, exactly as only the sign of the product matters.

Breaks: in chess white always moves first, so the colours are not perfectly symmetric. Coulomb's law has no such asymmetry at all.

Part 2 · Notes 1.3

The electric field E = F / q

Definition

Coulomb's law describes a pair of charges. Factor out the charge that feels the force, and what remains depends only on the source and the place: the field. It turns a two-body law into a property of every point in space.

E(P) = Fqtest = Q4πε0 · rr3r = rP − rQ points from source to P

The faint arrows map E around the source. Drag the small test charge q to any point P, then change q: the force arrow grows and shrinks with q, but the field arrow stays put.

The r3 trap: the readout shows |r| / r3, which always equals 1/r2. The field still falls as 1/r2. Writing r/r3 just saves you from turning r into a unit vector first. Field-map arrows show direction only; darker means stronger.

Key facts

  • E = F/q is well defined because F is proportional to q: the ratio does not depend on the probe.
  • Point charge: E = Q/(4πε0r2), radially away from +Q and towards −Q. Unit V/m.
  • r = rP − rQ points from the source to P. Writing r/r3 does not make the field fall as 1/r3.
  • The force on a positive charge points along E, on a negative charge against E.
  • The test charge must be small, so it does not move the source charges.
Analogy

E is to charge what g is to mass

Near the Earth, gravity's field is g = 9.81 N/kg. A 1 kg bag feels 9.81 N, a 10 kg bag feels 98.1 N, but g is the same: it describes the place, not the bag. Divide the weight by the mass and you always get g back. The electric field works exactly this way.

GravityElectric mass m (kg)→test charge q (C) weight F = m·g→force F = q·E g = F/m in N/kg→E = F/q in N/C = V/m the Earth→the source charge Q

Holds: the field is a property of the location, set by the source. The probe only reads it out. A heavier bag (bigger q) feels more force, not a stronger field.

Breaks: every mass falls down along g. A negative charge is pushed against E. Also g is nearly uniform in a room, while the field of a point charge changes strongly with r.

Why the test charge must be small: imagine measuring g with a second planet instead of a bag. The planet would pull the Earth too and change what you're measuring. A small q is the bag, not the planet.

Part 3 · Notes 1.4–1.7

Superposition, charge densities and field lines

AxiomDefinition

With several charges, each one produces its own field as if the others were not there. The total field is the vector sum. Nothing new is needed beyond Coulomb's law and this one rule.

E(P) = E1 + E2 + … = 14πε0 Σ Qiri3 riri = rP − rQi: one vector per charge, each from that charge to P

Drag the charges or the probe P. At P, each charge's own field is drawn in its colour, placed tip to tail, and the thick arrow is their sum. All arrows at P share one scale, so the vector addition is exact. Field lines are traced from the computed total field.

Charge densities [1.6]

Real charge is spread out. We describe how much sits on a small piece, then add (integrate) the pieces. Each piece acts like a point charge dQ, so superposition still does all the work.

Uniform cases need no integral: λ = Q/l, σ = Q/A, ρV = Q/V. When shrinking dA or dV to a point, every edge must shrink towards that point.

Key facts

  • Superposition: add the fields as vectors, never their sizes. The readout shows the difference. [1.4]
  • Charge densities: λ (C/m), σ (C/m2), ρV (C/m3); integrate to get Q. Each piece dQ acts as a point charge. [1.6]
  • Field lines: the tangent to a line at any point is the direction of E there. The arrows show the sense.
  • Lines start on positive charges and end on negative ones (or run off to infinity).
  • Lines never cross: a nonzero field has one direction. Where they would meet, E = 0 (look between the two equal charges).
  • Denser lines mean a stronger field. The number of lines leaving a charge is proportional to its charge: compare +2Q with −Q.
  • Lines meet a conductor's surface at right angles (Part 6, conductor mode, shows why).
Analogy

Several fans blowing in one room

Switch on two fans pointing at the same spot. The air velocity there is the vector sum of what each fan alone would produce. Turn one off, and the other's contribution is unchanged. Each charge is a fan; the field at a point is the combined breeze.

Holds: contributions add as vectors and do not disturb each other. Where two equal fans face each other, the air at the midpoint is still: that is the field-free point between two equal charges.

Breaks: air really moves and real airflows interfere (turbulence), so fans only add approximately. Electric fields add exactly, and nothing flows. A negative charge is a “fan that sucks”, which has no clean airflow equivalent.

Part 4 · Notes 1.8–1.9

Potential, equipotentials and voltage

TheoremDefinition

Moving a charge against the field costs work. Because the Coulomb force is central and depends only on r, that work depends only on the start and end points, never on the route. This theorem is what lets us give every point a single number, the potential.

We = −Q ∫P0P1 E · ds∮ E · ds = 0φ(P) = −∫P0P E · dsU12 = φ1 − φ2 = ∫P1P2 E · dsWe is the work supplied from outside. φ(P0) = 0 at the reference point (here: infinity). For a point charge φ = Q/(4πε0r).
φ lowφ highPoint at the map to read φ and |E| there.

The altitude profile: φ along each path

Map: colour is the potential (red high, blue low); thin curves are equipotentials, drawn at ±0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 V, so each step doubles. The dashed curve is φ = 0. Drag A, B, the bend point M or the charges. Profile: both paths start at φ(A) and end at φ(B), whatever happens in between. That is path independence, and the voltage UAB is the height difference between the two ends.

Sign of the work [1.8]

Move charge Q relative to Q0 Q0Q > 0 (repel) Q0Q < 0 (attract)
closer We > 0: you supply energy We < 0: the field does work
further away We < 0: the field does work We > 0: you supply energy
along an equipotential We = 0 We = 0

Key facts

  • Path independence: ∮ E·ds = 0, so the work from A to B depends only on A and B. This is what makes a potential possible. [1.8]
  • The reference point is a free choice (earth, ∞, circuit ground). It shifts every φ but no voltage.
  • Point charge with φ(∞) = 0: φ = Q/(4πε0r). It falls as 1/r, while E falls as 1/r2.
  • Voltage U12 = φ1 − φ2 = ∫ from P1 to P2 of E·ds. Swapping the indices flips the sign. [1.9]
  • Equipotentials: an equipotential is the set of points with the same φ. Moving along one costs no work.
  • They cross field lines at right angles, because E · ds = 0 along them. Turn on the field lines to check.
  • With equal φ-steps between lines, crowded lines mean a strong field.
  • A conductor's whole body is one equipotential, and its inside is field-free.
Analogy

A hiking map

Potential is altitude. Equipotential lines are the contour lines on the map. The field points straight downhill, and it is strongest where contour lines bunch together (a steep slope). Voltage is the height difference between two places.

Hiking mapElectrostatics altitude above sea level→potential φ sea level→reference point (earth or ∞) contour lines→equipotential lines steepest downhill direction→field E height gain between huts→voltage U

Holds: the height gained from hut A to hut B is the same by the short steep path or the long gentle one. Walking a loop back to the start gains nothing. Moving the “sea level” changes every altitude but no height difference.

Breaks: everything rolls downhill on a real hill. A negative charge “rolls uphill” in φ, because the force on it is −|Q|E.

Part 5 · Notes 1.10

Flux density and Gauss's law

DefinitionTheorem

The flux density D counts charge directly, and the material enters only through ε. Integrate D over a surface and you get the electric flux Ψ. Over a closed surface, the flux equals the charge inside: that is Gauss's law.

D = εE = ε0εrEΨ = ∬A D · dA∯A D · dA = QencD in C/m2, Ψ in C. dA = n dA points outward. Nothing flows: “flux” is a historical name.

Why exactly 1/r2 matters

Picture the influence of a charge as a fixed number of field lines spreading out. The surface they pass through is a sphere of area 4πr2. If the field falls as 1/r2, the product “field × area” is the same on every sphere: every line that leaves the charge crosses every sphere. Switch the law to 1/r3 and see what breaks.

Gauss's law at work: a charged cylinder [1.10.2]

Symmetry tells us D points radially and depends only on the distance ρ from the axis. Wrap a Gauss cylinder of radius ρ and length l around the axis: only its side face counts, and it has area 2πρl. So D · 2πρl = Qenc, and everything comes down to counting the enclosed charge.

Cylinder radius a = 1 m, uniform ρV = 1 µC/m3, per l = 1 m. Inside, the enclosed charge grows as ρ2 while the area grows as ρ, so D grows linearly. Outside, the charge is fixed and D falls as 1/ρ.

Recipe: 1 symmetry fixes the direction of D · 2 choose a surface where D is constant · 3 left side = D × area · 4 right side = enclosed charge · 5 solve, then E = D/ε

Key facts

  • Gauss's law: the flux of D out of any closed surface equals the enclosed charge. Charges outside add zero net flux.
  • It holds because the field falls exactly as 1/r2; it is valid in all of electrodynamics.
  • It gives the field directly only with symmetry: sphere, infinite cylinder, infinite plane.
  • Why two field quantities: the flux of D counts the free charge whatever the material; E is what a charge feels, reduced by εr. In vacuum they differ only by ε0.
  • Results: point charge D = Q/(4πr2); charged cylinder D = ρVρ/2 inside, ρVa2/(2ρ) outside.
Analogy

A light bulb in the dark

A 60 W bulb sends out a fixed amount of light. Wrap it in an imaginary sphere: all the light passes through that sphere, whatever its size. A sphere twice as large has four times the area, so each square metre gets a quarter of the light. So twice as far away, a square metre of wall receives a quarter of the light.

LampCharge power of the bulb (W)→charge Q inside light arriving per m2→field strength D (or E) total light through the sphere→flux Ψ = Q a second bulb inside adds its light→superposition

Holds: total through any closed surface = what is inside, independent of the surface's size or shape. A bulb outside the sphere adds nothing in total: its light goes in one side and out the other. Same for a charge outside a Gauss surface.

Breaks: light really travels and carries energy away. In electrostatics nothing flows; “flux” is only a count of field lines, a historical name. Also light has no “negative bulb” that swallows light, while a negative charge is exactly where field lines end.

The 1/r3 mode is like light that gets absorbed by fog on the way: the total through a bigger sphere shrinks, so you can no longer read off the bulb's power from any sphere. Clear air (1/r2) is what makes Gauss's law work.

Part 6 · Notes 1.11

Interface conditions and the ideal plate capacitor

Theorem

At the boundary between two materials, split each field into a normal part (across the boundary) and a tangential part (along it). Two tools fix what happens: Gauss's law on a flat pillbox gives Dn, and the closed loop integral on a thin rectangle gives Et.

Dn2 − Dn1 = σEt1 = Et2tan α1tan α2 = ε1ε2σ = free surface charge on the boundary (0 when no charge sits on it); the normal points from medium 1 into medium 2. α = angle between the field and the normal.

The shading darkens with εr. Dashed lines split each vector into its normal part (along the dashed normal) and its tangential part (along the boundary). Switch to D to see which component is continuous for D.

The ideal plate capacitor by superposition [1.11.4]

One infinite sheet with +σ alone sends D = σ/2 away from itself on both sides. The −σ sheet sends σ/2 towards itself. Add them region by region:

Between the plates: D = σ, E = σ/(ε0εr), homogeneous, pointing from + to −. Outside: zero. Hence U = E · d. Real plates have fringing at the edges; the course neglects it.

Key facts

  • Rule: Dn jumps only by free surface charge; Et is always continuous. The other two components follow from D = εE.
  • Going into the material with larger ε, the field bends away from the normal, and E gets weaker.
  • At a conductor, E = 0 inside, so Et = 0 just outside too: field lines arrive at 90°, and Dn = σ, E = σ/ε0.
  • Ideal plate capacitor: D = σ and E = σ/(ε0εr) between the plates, zero outside, so U = E·d. [1.11.4]
Analogy

Light bending into water

A light ray entering water bends at the surface; the notes make the same comparison. Field lines bend at a jump in ε, and the amount follows from which component must stay continuous.

Holds: a direction change at a boundary, set by a material property on each side, and no bending at normal incidence (α1 = 0).

Breaks: light obeys Snell's law with sines and refractive index. The field obeys a tangent law with ε. And at a water surface part of the light is reflected; the field has no reflected part.

Part 7 · Notes 1.12

Matter in the field: induction and polarization

Theorem

Slide a slab into a charged, disconnected capacitor (Q fixed, so D = σ in the air and in a dielectric; inside a conductor D = 0). A conductor's free electrons move until its inside is field-free. A dielectric has no free charges, but its molecules stretch or turn into the field and weaken it by εr. Either way the voltage drops.

D = ε0E + PP = ε0χEεr = 1 + χp = QdP: dipole moment per volume. χ: susceptibility. d points from − to + in each dipole.

Gap d = 10 mm, empty-gap field E0 = 100 kV/m, so Ua = 1000 V. The voltage is the area under the E(x) curve. Conductor: E = 0 in the slab. Dielectric: E = E0/εr in the slab. So Uconductor < Udielectric < Uempty.

Key facts

  • Induction: the induced surface charge equals the arriving flux density, σ = D. Separate the two halves inside the field and both stay charged.
  • Shielding: an empty cavity inside a conductor is field-free, whatever the outside field (Faraday cage). A charge +Q inside a hollow sphere induces −Q on the inner wall and +Q on the outer wall.
  • Polarization: displacement (electron clouds or ions shift) or orientation (existing dipoles like H2O turn). Bound charges on the slab faces weaken the field inside.
  • Vacuum and air: εr = 1, χ = 0.
Analogy

A crowd in a hall versus an audience in seats

A conductor is a crowd standing in an open hall. Push from the left, and people walk to the far wall until nobody feels a push any more. A dielectric is an audience bolted into seats. Push them and they can only lean; the leaning partly cancels the push but can't remove it.

CrowdMatter people walk to the walls→free electrons move to the surface nobody pushed any more→E = 0 inside the conductor seated people lean→dipoles stretch or rotate how far they can lean→χ, and εr = 1 + χ

Holds: free movers cancel the push completely; bound movers only partly. Both leave “charge” piled at the surfaces.

Breaks: people decide to move; charges simply follow the force. And in a conductor the charges stop exactly when E = 0, not when they are tired.

Part 8 · Notes 1.13–1.14

Capacitors, networks and stored energy

DefinitionTheorem

Two conductors with +Q and −Q: all flux runs from one to the other. Double Q and every field doubles, so U doubles too. Q and U are proportional, and the constant is the capacitance, set by geometry and material only.

C = QU = ∯ D·dA∫ E·dsWe = ½CU2 = ½QU = Q22Cwe = ½E·DRecipe for any shape: put ±Q on the electrodes → D by Gauss → E = D/ε → U = ∫E·ds → C = Q/U (Q cancels).

Capacitance of the three standard shapes

Move the sliders: the sketch scales with the dimensions and the formula fills in your numbers. The shading of the gap darkens with εr.

Networks: series and parallel [1.13.3]

Series: same charge on each (induction passes it along), voltages add, so 1/C adds. Parallel: same voltage, charges add, so C adds. The smaller capacitor in a series pair takes the larger voltage. This is the reverse of resistors.

Where the energy formula comes from [1.14]

While charging, the voltage grows with the charge already there: u = q/C. Moving the next small dq across that voltage costs dW = u·dq (the thin strip). Add all strips: the triangle under the line, area ½·Q·U. The first charges were cheap, the last ones expensive, so the energy is half of Q·U, not Q·U.

Key facts

  • C depends only on geometry and material, never on Q or U. Unit farad: 1 F = 1 As/V.
  • Plate: ε0εrA/d. Sphere: 4πε ab/(b − a). Coax: 2πεl/ln(b/a). Multi-layer with n plates per terminal: (2n − 1)εA/d.
  • Parallel: same U, C adds. Series: same Q, 1/C adds. The reverse of resistors.
  • We = ½CU2 = ½QU = Q2/(2C); energy density we = ½E·D in J/m3.
  • Whether Q or U is held fixed decides how E, U and We respond to a change. Part 9 compares both.
Analogy

A water tank filled from below

Pump water into a tank through a pipe at the bottom. The water level rises with the volume inside; a wider tank rises less for the same volume. Each new litre must be pushed against the pressure of the water already there.

TankCapacitor volume of water→charge Q water level (pressure)→voltage U tank's floor area→capacitance C volume = area × level→Q = C·U energy ½·ρg·A·h2→energy ½·C·U2

Holds: Q = CU, the ½ in the energy (early water was lifted less), and networks: tanks side by side at the same level add their areas (parallel).

Breaks: a capacitor holds +Q and −Q, so its total charge is zero; a tank really holds water. Series capacitors have no clean tank picture. The notes also use a spring (C ↔ 1/spring constant, U ↔ force), which carries the energy formula equally well.

Part 10 · Whole chapter

Check your reasoning

Commit to an answer in your head before opening each one.

1. Where can the field be zero? (+2 nC at x = 0, −1 nC at x = 3 m)

Argue region by region. Between the charges, +2 nC pushes a test charge right and −1 nC pulls it right: both fields point +x, so they add and cannot cancel. Left of the origin, the two fields point opposite ways, but you are closer to the larger charge, so it always wins. Right of x = 3 m the fields point opposite ways, and here you are closer to the smaller charge. Its closeness can balance the other's size. So the zero lies right of 3 m.

Exact point. 2/x2 = 1/(x − 3)2 gives √2·(x − 3) = x, so x = 3√2/(√2 − 1) ≈ 10.24 m. The plot shows E along the axis (vertical axis compressed with asinh so the spikes fit).

General rule for two point charges: with opposite signs, the zero on their connecting line lies outside them, beyond the smaller charge. With equal signs it lies between them, closer to the smaller one.

2. “E = F/q depends on q, so a bigger test charge changes the field.” Where is the flaw?

The argument treats F as fixed while q changes. It is not: by Coulomb's law F is itself proportional to q. Double q and F doubles, so the ratio F/q stays the same. Part 2 shows this directly. The real caveat is different: a large test charge can push the source charges around (in a conductor, for example), which changes the field you are trying to measure. That is why the test charge must be small, not because of the division.

3. If the force fell as 1/r3, what survives and what breaks?

Survives: the definition E = F/q (F would still be proportional to q). Superposition. Path independence and the potential: those only need the force to be central and depend on r alone. Any function of r would do.

Breaks: Gauss's law. Field × sphere area would be proportional to 1/r, so the flux would depend on the size of the sphere you draw, not only on the charge inside (Part 5, hypothetical mode). Everything built on Gauss's law breaks with it: the interface condition for Dn, the plate-capacitor field, and the capacitance formulas.

4. Path 1 from A to B is short, path 2 is a long detour. Which needs more work to carry a charge along?

Neither. The work is Q(φB − φA), which depends only on the end points because ∮E·ds = 0 in electrostatics. Part 4 measures both integrals numerically: they agree. A long path just has more segments where the field helps and more where it hinders, and they cancel to the same total.

5. A charge Q sits at the centre of a cube. What is the flux through one face? What if Q sits just outside the cube?

Inside: total flux Ψ = Q through the closed cube, and by symmetry each of the 6 faces carries Q/6. Outside: the enclosed charge is zero, so the total flux through the cube is zero. Lines enter on one side and leave on the other (the light-bulb picture: a bulb outside the sphere adds nothing in total).

6. A field leaves air at α1 = 45° into a dielectric with εr = 4. What is α2, and is E stronger or weaker inside?

tan α2 = tan α1 · ε2/ε1 = 1 · 4 = 4, so α2 ≈ 76°: the field bends away from the normal. Et stays the same and En drops to ¼, so |E| is weaker: |E2|/|E1| = √(0.5 + 0.5/16) ≈ 0.73. Set εr1 = 1, εr2 = 4, α1 = 45° in Part 6 to check.

7. A disconnected capacitor (U = 1000 V) gets a metal slab filling half the gap. What is the new voltage?

Q is fixed, so D and the field in the remaining air are unchanged. The metal half contributes nothing to ∫E·ds. So U halves to 500 V, and C = Q/U doubles. A dielectric slab of the same thickness with εr = 3 would give 500 + 500/3 ≈ 667 V. Part 7 shows both.

8. You double the plate gap d of a charged capacitor. What happens to E, U and We (a) disconnected, (b) still connected to the source?

(a) Q fixed: σ, D and E stay the same; U = E·d doubles; C halves; We = ½QU doubles. The extra energy comes from your hands pulling the attracting plates apart.

(b) U fixed: E = U/d halves; D, σ and Q halve; C halves; We = ½CU2 halves. Check both in Part 9 with the d row of the matrix.

9. C1 = 2 µF and C2 = 3 µF in series, parallel with C3 = 4 µF, on 10 V. Find Ctotal, U1 and U2.

Series pair: 2·3/(2+3) = 1.2 µF. Total: 1.2 + 4 = 5.2 µF. The pair holds Q = 1.2 µF · 10 V = 12 µC on each capacitor, so U1 = 12/2 = 6 V and U2 = 12/3 = 4 V (they add to 10 V). These are the default values of the network in Part 8.
